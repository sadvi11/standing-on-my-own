---
title: My body knew before I did
lede: For years I did not say what I thought. I assumed the cost was emotional. It turned up in my chest and my shoulders long before I understood what it was.
date: 2026-09-09
kind: note
---

For most of my life I did not say what I actually thought.

Not because I had nothing to say. Because everyone else's feelings seemed to
matter more, and somewhere along the way I stopped counting mine at all. If
someone would be upset, I did not say it. If saying it would cause a problem, I
did not say it. If I was not sure it was worth a problem, I did not say it.

Almost nothing is worth a problem, once you start deciding that way.

I thought I was being good. Easy to be around. The one who does not make things
difficult.

## What it actually cost

I was unhappy for a long time without being able to name why. My confidence was
low in a way that did not match anything about my life on paper — I had studied
hard, I had built a career, I had done things that should have made me
confident. None of it reached the part of me that believed my opinion was the
one thing not worth the trouble.

And it showed up in my body first.

My chest would go heavy and tight. My posture folded in on itself — shoulders
forward, made smaller, the shape of someone taking up as little room as
possible. I did not connect these things to anything. I thought I was tired.

## The sentence that reorganised it

Then I read *The Body Keeps the Score* by Dr Bessel van der Kolk.

I am not going to summarise it, and I am not qualified to explain the science.
What it did for me was smaller and more specific than that: it gave me a way to
consider that the thing in my chest and the thing in my life might not be two
separate problems.

That was the shift. Not a technique. Just the possibility that my body had been
keeping a record I had not been reading.

## What changed after

I started treating my own needs as real information rather than as an
inconvenience to be managed. I noticed when I agreed with something because I
agreed, and when I agreed because disagreeing felt dangerous. I began saying
small true things out loud and surviving the discomfort.

My mind got quieter. Not immediately, and not completely. But the constant
background calculation of what everyone else needed — that got quieter, and
there turned out to be a lot of room underneath it.

The changes in my life since have been larger than I expected from something
that started so small.

---

**One thing I have to say plainly.** Chest pain is a medical symptom and it
needs a doctor, not a book and not an essay. I am not a therapist or a
physician, and I am not suggesting that what worked for me is treatment for
anyone else. If something in your body is telling you something, please have it
looked at properly first. What I am describing is what I understood afterwards,
not instead.
